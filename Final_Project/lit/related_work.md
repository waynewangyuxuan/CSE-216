# Related work

Draft for Milestone 2. Selection follows `protocol.md`. The seed papers predate that protocol, and the search was built afterwards.

**How each source was read:** **[F]** read through in full. **[R]** full text in hand, relevant sections read, not cover to cover. **[A]** abstract only. **[A+]** abstract plus the named part. **[S]** second-hand, through another paper's use of it. Search finds not marked otherwise were screened from title and abstract only. Sources with no published version found are marked **(preprint)** or **(repository)**; that check used Crossref title search, so a missing mark is reliable and a present one may be out of date.

## 1. Offloading and surrender as concepts

Cognitive offloading began as a neutral term. Risko & Gilbert (2016) [F, accepted manuscript] define it as physical action that changes a task's processing demands to reduce cognitive demand, and propose that a metacognitive evaluation of internal and external strategies drives the choice. They note that this choice need not be conscious, that offloading has both costs and benefits, and that reliance can drift over time. They also say its benefit for learning depends on whether the demand removed is needed for the learning goal.

Shaw & Nave (2026) [R] (preprint) separate strategic delegation from "cognitive surrender", in which a person adopts AI output with minimal scrutiny, and add AI as a third system next to intuition and deliberation. In three preregistered experiments on Cognitive Reflection Test items (N = 1,372), accuracy rose when the AI was right and fell when it was wrong. The authors treat that pattern as the behavioral signature of surrender, and also say surrender is not inherently irrational. Their task is not writing, and they note limits on generalizing.

The vocabulary is not settled. PAUSE (Alam 2026) [R] (preprint) uses "offloading" for routinely substituting LLM output for one's own reasoning. Maier et al. (2026) [R] (preprint) name "answer offloading" as the negative form of offloading. Zhu et al. (2026) [A+ limitations] draw the line inside offloading, between dependent and autonomous offloading, using a three-wave survey, and stress that all their outcomes are self-perceived. T. Kim et al. (2025) [A] propose two dimensions, division of cognitive labor and metacognitive oversight, giving four patterns, one of them named Cognitive Surrender. Hwang (2026) [A] (repository) argues the danger of surrender holds even when the tool is right. **The offloading-versus-surrender pairing in this project's RQ1 is therefore one stipulation among several, not a settled distinction.**

Related work asks whether people can see this happening. Skulmowski (2024) [A] discusses placebo and ghostwriter effects, where users misjudge or do not disclose AI's contribution. H. Kim et al. (2026) [A] (preprint) propose an "LLM fallacy" of reading assisted output as one's own competence. Tankelevitch et al. (2024) [A] frame generative AI use as a metacognitive demand. Bhat et al. (2026) [A] report, from 19 interviews and 1,291 sessions, that suggested ideas seeded writers' directions while writers did not notice the influence. This bears directly on RQ2, though none of these studies tests what is visible at the level of a single interaction.

## 2. Tracing AI-assisted writing

Several studies now code behavior from logs, which is the project's own method.

**Essay and classroom traces.** NIRVANA (Jelson et al. 2026a) [R] (preprint) records keystrokes, ChatGPT conversations and paste events from 77 students writing a roughly 30-minute argumentative essay, and clusters writers into four profiles using two ratios, human contribution and human edits. The authors show the limits of those metrics: P26 clusters as a Collaborator, but on qualitative replay looks more like an editor of AI text. Jelson et al. (2026b) [R] code the queries from what appears to be the same collection (same group, also n = 77; neither paper section I read states this outright) against Flower & Hayes's processes, reporting Cohen's κ = .89. **Treat these two as one data source.** Yang et al. (2025) [A] find four temporal patterns linked to knowledge telling and transforming. J. Kim et al. (2025) [A] combine think-aloud, screen recording and chat from 36 graduate students. Woo et al. (2025) [A] (preprint) find 15 kinds of edit to AI text from screen recordings of 29 secondary students.

**Who directs the work.** Poquet et al. (2026) [R] are closest to this project. In a synthesis-writing task with 97 students, they code each prompt as active or passive, by whether the learner directs the work, and as local or global in scope. They find this content view separates learners in ways volume of use does not. Their authors caution that cluster sizes limit power. Chen (2026) [R] scores surface, structural, idea and reasoning offloading from prompts, outputs, adoption decisions and revisions, in an eight-week quasi-experiment with 168 completers. In associative analyses, idea and reasoning offloading had the largest negative estimates for later no-AI thinking. With six classes, the author treats significance as fragile.

**At scale and in the wild.** Mysore et al. (2025) [A+ introduction, data section] start from 20.5 million Bing Copilot and 800,000 WildChat sessions, filter to about 250,000 and 68,000 multi-turn writing sessions, and find in their follow-up turns a small set of behaviors: revising intents, exploring texts, asking questions, adjusting style, injecting content. WildChat (Zhao et al. 2024) [R] is the public source. Mallissery (2026) [A] (repository; code and results for a manuscript I did not find published) proposes a Reliance Index from 16,851 student-LLM interactions in the StudyChat dataset, and states that it describes behavior without establishing surrender. It is close to this project's RQ3, and its evidence is thin.

**Keystroke-only and related datasets.** ScholaWrite (Le et al. 2026) [R] has no AI interaction, but gives 15 intention labels from five computer science preprints, and finds that more than half of sessions blend three or more intentions. RECIPE4U (Han et al. 2024) [A] covers 212 EFL students revising with ChatGPT, with 13 intent labels and edit histories; it has student identity and uptake, which WildChat lacks, but is language-learning revision, not research writing.

## 3. Consequences for learning and thinking

**Evidence that AI use can reduce what remains unaided.** In a field experiment with nearly a thousand high school students, Bastani et al. (2025) [A+ introduction] find that unguarded GPT-4 raised practice performance but students could underperform once it was removed, while a tutor giving hints mitigated this. Contractor & Reyes (2026) [A+ introduction] (preprint) find AI access raised unaided test scores, with gains lasting for students using it as a tutor and fading for students having it write. Kosmyna et al. (2025) [A] (preprint) report weaker EEG connectivity and lower ownership for LLM users, with 54 participants and 18 in the crossover session, so this evidence is small and unreviewed. Georgiou (2025) [A] (preprint) reports lower scores on a cognitive engagement scale in a ChatGPT condition.

**Evidence that the answer depends on how it is used.** Maier et al. (2026) [R] (preprint, N = 704) find no evidence that AI access harmed later performance on fraction arithmetic, when full answers required an explicit request. Requesting complete answers more often was associated with worse tests, and metacognitive feedback reduced such requests. The authors note the design limits generalization. So Bastani and Maier differ on access, and they may not conflict, because their assistants handled answers differently. Doshi & Hauser (2024) [A] show a different cost: AI ideas improved individual stories but made stories more alike.

**Self-report.** Lee et al. (2025) [R], surveying 319 knowledge workers, report that higher confidence in AI goes with less perceived critical thinking, and describe a shift toward verification, integration and stewardship. These are self-reports, as the paper's own subtitle says.

**This body of work is not about research writing,** and most outcomes are short-term. The project also promised not to measure skill change, so it can only cite these findings when interpreting risk, not confirm them.

## 4. Checking, verification and uptake

Peters & Chin-Yee (2025) [A+ preprint opening] show that LLM summaries of science overgeneralize: 4,900 summaries compared with sources, and LLM summaries were nearly five times as likely as human ones to contain broad generalizations. This shows why checks matter but measures models, not writers, which is why both project screeners excluded it.

Tao et al. (2026) [R] (preprint) plant flawed suggestions in a real course writing task (N = 129). Requiring written justification cut adoption of flawed suggestions from 65% to 41%, without cutting acceptance of sound ones, yet a thematic analysis found the justifications superficial. That matters here: **a stated reason is not by itself good evidence of careful evaluation.** Ghosh et al. (2026) [A] (preprint) test cognitive forcing functions on AI plans, and find one reduced overreliance without raising load. Welzel & Vincent (2026) [A] use text overlap between AI suggestions and writing as an observable measure.

Nogueira et al. (2026) [A] report that researchers using LLMs for literature reviews face a persistent verification burden. Wu (2026) [A], from interviews with 21 doctoral students, is framed around a move from production to verification.

Measures of reliance include Padmakumar et al. (2026) [R] (preprint), who estimate the fraction of planning, execution and verification steps handed over, using counterfactual workflows validated with 40 developers on programming. Hossain & Nawmi (2026) [A+ constructs] (preprint) offer a validated 20-item survey of reliance types in academic writing. Davalos & Zhang (2026) [A] (preprint) look for temporal signatures of AI assistance in keystrokes, partly with simulated delegation.

## 5. Ownership and agency

Reza et al. (2025) [A+ introduction] review 109 HCI papers and interview 15 writers, finding four design strategies (structured guidance, guided exploration, active co-writing, critical feedback), and that content-focused writers such as academics prioritize ownership during planning. Joshi & Vogel (2025) [A] find AI lowers psychological ownership and longer prompts help. Park et al. (2026) [A] follow 302 participants turn by turn: self-efficacy tends to fall and trust to rise, and those who lose self-efficacy more often ask the LLM to edit directly. Christenson et al. (2026) [A] (workshop paper; pilot of 24) find limited access raised reported ownership relative to unlimited access. Yukita et al. (2025) [A] (preprint) argue that delegating writing turns planning, translating and reviewing into planning, waiting and reviewing. Wan et al. (2024) [A] place 34 writing systems on a taxonomy of cognitive contributions adapted from Flower & Hayes.

Felt ownership and observable contribution are different things. Jelson et al. (2026b) report that students relying on ChatGPT for planning or reviewing felt comparable ownership, and the authors flag this as a concern. So ownership self-reports cannot stand in for the project's behavioral codes, or the reverse.

These sources were not found by the search, because of its date limit. All are cited through how others use them unless marked.

Flower & Hayes (1981) [S] supply planning, translating and reviewing, and the monitoring that coordinates them; Jelson (2026b) and Reza (2025) both build on this. Bereiter & Scardamalia (1987) [not read] are the codebook's source for knowledge telling and transforming, terms Yang (2025) also uses in the abstract. Sweller et al. ## 7. What the literature leaves open

Stated as gaps in what this search found, not gaps proven to exist:

1. **Research writing with AI traces.** Inclusion was low for Q5 (10 of 65 records). What was found is interviews or user studies (Wu 2026; Nogueira 2026), or keystrokes with no AI (ScholaWrite). NIRVANA and most trace studies use essays or classroom tasks.
2. **Grounds of checking, read from traces.** Tao (2026) measures adoption of planted flaws and reads justifications, and Peters (2025) studies models. I found none that grades, from naturalistic traces, whether a check used independent evidence.
3. **Multiple fields in a single window.** Poquet and Chen code who directs and at what level. Mysore codes follow-ups. NIRVANA measures uptake. I did not find one scheme coding request, decision, check and uptake together, though the search was bounded, so one may exist.
4. **Reliability of this kind of coding.** Reported agreement exists for narrower schemes (Jelson's κ = .89 on four process codes; expert-LLM κ = 0.64 and 0.61 on Poquet's prompt coding). No equivalent exists for a scheme this wide, and that is a risk for the project.

**What this means for novelty.** The course does not require novelty, and that is fortunate. By late 2026 this area is crowded and moving fast: 2026 papers split offloading by dependence, direction and layer. The project's realistic contribution is integrating these observable distinctions for research writing, and honestly reporting where logs cannot support them, rather than introducing the distinction itself.
